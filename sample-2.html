About Sagework

Health is shaped by systems.

Biology does not operate in isolation. Nutrition, the gut microbiome, the built environment and human behaviour interact continuously, influencing health, resilience and performance.

Sagework is an evidence-led advisory and design practice working across biology, environment and behaviour. We bring these forces into one evidence-led framework and translate research into practical strategy, system design and implementation for organisations, institutions and leaders addressing complex human challenges.

Discover Sagework →

The SAGE Triad

Three domains. One human system.

Biology, environment and behaviour are not separate variables. Sagework examines how these domains interact, identifies where the system is working against people and designs interventions that can be applied in the real world.

Biology responds to conditions. Environments influence behaviour. Behaviour reinforces outcomes.

Sustainable change requires all three to be considered together.

    The present need

    Modern systems are changing faster than human biology.

    Conceptual illustration, not plotted data.

    Artificial light extends the day. Digital systems compete for attention. Food environments prioritise convenience. Buildings can disconnect people from movement, nature and restorative rhythms.

    Work and education often demand continuous performance without creating the conditions that sustain it. This mismatch is associated with disrupted sleep, fatigue, persistent stress, reduced concentration, poorer metabolic health and slower recovery.

    Sagework examines this as the Human Adaptation Gap: the distance between what modern systems demand from people and what human biology needs to function well.

    Many health solutions focus on the individual after the effects appear. Sagework begins with the system itself. People do not live in silos. Nutrition cannot be separated from the environment in which people eat. Behaviour cannot be separated from the biological, social and structural conditions that make it possible. Space is not simply a backdrop. It is an active input into how people feel, behave and recover.

    Sagework reconnects these parts through science, systems thinking and design.

    The Five Senses

    How the environment communicates with the body.

    Every environment places demands on the people within it. Light, sound, smell, taste and touch shape how we experience a space, direct our attention and respond to what is around us. Try each one below.

    01 · Sight

    Light that supports the activity.

    Light supports vision and also carries information about time of day. Glare, contrast, visual complexity and access to daylight all deserve attention when designing a space.

    Day · Evening · Night

    An international expert consensus provides separate recommendations for daytime, evening and night-time light exposure in healthy adults, supporting light as a timed biological input.1

    Source: PLOS Biology

    For organisations, the starting point is to assess lighting where people actually work, learn or rest, then address glare and task visibility.

    09:00Morning light

    02 · Sound

    Acoustics with consequences.

    Sound can support communication or compete with it. Its source, duration and predictability matter, alongside its volume.

    8%

    Higher relative risk of developing ischaemic heart disease for each 10 dB increase in long-term road-traffic noise exposure, in a WHO-commissioned systematic review. This population-level association is specific to that exposure and outcome.2

    Source: WHO-commissioned systematic review

    Within organisations, we examine avoidable noise, speech clarity, privacy and the separation of activities that make competing acoustic demands.

    SilenceChoose a setting to listen. Use a comfortable volume.

    03 · Smell

    The biology of scent and association.

    Smell helps us recognise food, places and familiar experiences. It is closely connected with memory and emotion, yet responses to the same odour can differ considerably. A screen cannot carry scent, so notice what your memory supplies as you choose each one.

    2004

    Richard Axel and Linda Buck received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discoveries explaining odour receptors and the organisation of the olfactory system.3

    Source: nobelprize.org

    In practice, Sagework begins with odour sources, ventilation and the needs of the people exposed. A pleasant scent alone does not establish a health benefit.

    RainPetrichor: the smell of earth after the first rain.

    04 · Taste

    Food choices begin with the food environment.

    Flavour combines taste with smell, texture, temperature and expectation. Food choice also depends on what is available, affordable, convenient and culturally familiar.

    11.5%

    Reduction in calories purchased from targeted food categories when availability and portion sizes changed, in a randomised trial across 19 workplace cafeterias. The study measured purchases, not consumption or weight loss.4

    Source: PLOS

    Sagework connects sensory appeal with nutrition, food provision and operational feasibility, considering the needs of the population being served.

    Sweet

    05 · Touch

    Contact and physical comfort.

    We encounter environments through seating, surfaces, materials and movement. Temperature and airflow add to the experience of physical comfort.

    137 studies

    In a 2024 meta-analysis, defined touch interventions showed benefits for outcomes including pain and anxiety. Those findings concern the interventions studied, not a general effect of interior materials. The 2021 Nobel Prize recognised discoveries concerning temperature and touch receptors.6,5

    Source: Nature Human Behaviour · nobelprize.org

    For environmental design, we examine usability, physical comfort and opportunities for adjustment. Where interpersonal touch is relevant to care, consent, training and context are essential.

    SilkPress and drag across the surface.

    The senses work together

    A space is experienced as a whole.

    Light, conversation, temperature, odour and physical comfort can reinforce one another or create competing demands. The same setting may also affect people differently, by age, health, sensory sensitivity and task.

    The SAGE Triad: three connected lenses

    Biology

    Considers the people receiving sensory input and how their needs and responses may differ.

    Environment

    Examines the conditions they encounter, including light, sound, odour, food and physical comfort.

    Behaviour

    Considers what those conditions encourage, interrupt or make easier to sustain.

    From understanding to implementation

    A change is useful when it can be implemented and evaluated.

    Depending on the setting: lighting adjustments, acoustic zoning, odour-source management, changes to food provision or greater personal control over physical comfort.

    Measures may include task visibility, speech intelligibility, reported comfort, food purchasing patterns, satisfaction and food waste. Clinical or productivity outcomes require an evaluation designed to test those outcomes directly.

    A journey through the gut

    From first bite to the brain.

    01 · Stomach

    Food meets acid.

    Acid and enzymes begin breaking down proteins, and muscular contractions churn food into a semi-liquid that is released gradually into the small intestine.

    02 · Small intestine

    Where most nutrients are absorbed.

    Its lining is folded into villi and microvilli, creating a vast surface through which most nutrients pass into the bloodstream.

    03 · Colon

    Home to most gut microbes.

    The colon hosts the densest community of gut microbes. They ferment fibre the body cannot digest on its own, producing compounds such as short-chain fatty acids.

    04 · Gut–brain axis

    A conversation in both directions.

    The gut and brain communicate through the vagus nerve, hormones, immune signals and microbial metabolites. What this means for mood and behaviour is an active area of research, and much remains uncertain.

    How Dr Gutsy approaches a question

    Listen. Investigate. Explain. Connect.

    Step 1

    Listen

    Begin with a question or experience people recognise, taking its context seriously rather than rushing to an explanation.

    Step 2

    Investigate

    Examine the evidence, question assumptions and distinguish what is established from what remains uncertain.

    Step 3

    Explain

    Use clear language, relatable stories and visual explanations to make the science understandable without losing its meaning.

    Step 4

    Connect

    Show what the evidence may mean in everyday life, what it does not establish and when a question needs qualified professional care.

    What you can expect

    • Questions drawn from everyday life.
    • Scientific explanations in clear, accessible language.
    • Stories and visuals that make complex ideas easier to follow.
    • Practical observations and questions to inform everyday decisions.
    • Visible sources and honest explanations of uncertainty.
    Clear science. Honest boundaries.

    Dr Gutsy is grounded in evidence-led science. We explain what research supports, where uncertainty remains and why findings may not apply equally to everyone.

    The platform provides health education. It does not diagnose conditions, prescribe treatment or replace individual medical care.

    We do not use fear, shame or promises of a cure to make an explanation more compelling. Where a question requires personal assessment, we encourage qualified professional care.
